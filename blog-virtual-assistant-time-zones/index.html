Working with a virtual assistant across time zones

If you run a business in London or New York and your support is in Israel, the time difference is a logistics detail, not an obstacle. Here is what actually changes, and the four things worth agreeing before you start.

Time zones are a scheduling problem, not a support problem

If you run a business in London or New York and you are thinking about support based in Israel, the first question is usually the same: will the time difference get in the way? The honest answer is that it can, if nobody plans for it, and that it is one of the easiest problems to solve if somebody does.

Here is how it actually works in practice, and what to agree up front so it never becomes an issue.

The gaps are smaller than people think

Israel runs two hours ahead of the UK and seven ahead of New York for most of the year. Both countries shift their clocks on slightly different dates, so for a few weeks in spring and autumn the gap moves by an hour. Worth knowing, not worth worrying about.

What that means day to day: a UK morning is an Israeli late morning. There is a wide, comfortable overlap. With the US East Coast the overlap is narrower but real, roughly the American morning against the Israeli afternoon and early evening. With the West Coast it is tighter still, and you work asynchronously more of the time.

The bigger difference is the shape of the week

This one catches people out more than the clock does. The Israeli working week runs Sunday to Thursday. Friday is a short day at most, and Saturday is not a working day at all.

So if your business runs Monday to Friday, you gain a day and lose a day. Sunday, which is dead time in the UK and the US, is a full working day here. Things get done while your competitors' offices are shut. Friday afternoon onwards is quiet, and nothing moves until Sunday morning.

For most businesses this is a net gain, not a loss. You just have to know it, so you are not sending something at four o'clock on a Friday and wondering why it is still sitting there.

Chagim and the yearly rhythm

The Jewish festivals cluster, particularly in the autumn, and they take out working days in a way that a standard calendar does not show. The stretch around Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur and Sukkot is the biggest one; Pesach is the other.

If you work with someone in Israel, or with an observant assistant anywhere, the fix is straightforward: get the dates on the shared calendar at the start of the year, and plan the work around them rather than into them. Deadlines that fall in those weeks get pulled forward. Nobody is surprised, nothing is late.

I do this with every client at the outset. It takes twenty minutes once and removes an entire category of awkward conversation.

Why the overlap matters less than you would guess

The instinct is to want your assistant online at the same time as you. In reality, most support work does not need that. Inbox triage, follow-up, document work, trackers, scheduling: none of it requires the two of you to be typing at the same moment.

What it does require is that the handover is clean. If I know what you want, what "done" looks like, and what to do when something unexpected turns up, then the fact that you are asleep when I am working is an advantage. You send at the end of your day; it is finished when you wake up.

The overlap you actually need is a reliable window, maybe an hour, where you can both talk properly if something needs deciding. Not the whole day.

Four things worth agreeing at the start

  • Your working days, and mine. Written down, including which days are short and which are off. No assumptions.
  • What counts as urgent. Most things are not. Agree what genuinely warrants a WhatsApp outside hours, and everything else waits for the next working morning without anyone feeling ignored.
  • One place for the work. A shared tracker, a shared folder, a running list. Somewhere either of us can look and see the current state without asking.
  • A regular check-in. Twenty or thirty minutes at a fixed time each week, in the overlap. This is where the questions that have been piling up get answered in one go, instead of dribbling out as interruptions.

The advantage nobody mentions

Working with someone in a different time zone forces a discipline that most small businesses never get to otherwise: you have to write things down. What you want, how it should be done, what happened. That documentation is genuinely valuable on its own, and it is why businesses that work this way often end up better organised than ones where everyone sits in the same room and nothing is ever recorded.

There is also the cultural piece, which matters more than time zones do. If you are an English-speaking business owner, whether in Israel, the UK or the US, working with someone who shares your language, your calendar and your way of doing things removes a layer of friction. You do not have to explain what erev chag means, or why nothing is happening on the second day of Rosh Hashanah. That is not a small thing when the whole point of getting support was to reduce the number of things you have to explain.

In short

The time difference is a logistics detail. Agree the days, agree what is urgent, keep one shared place for the work, and talk once a week. Do that and the distance stops being a factor within a fortnight.
