What makes a small business website actually work

Most small business websites fail at the same three points, and usually not the one the owner expects. Here is what to check, and what to fix first if your site is already live.

Most small business websites fail at the same three points

A visitor lands on your site and gives it a few seconds to answer three questions: what is this, is it for someone like me, and what do I do next. Sites that answer all three do their job. Sites that do not, however handsome they are, quietly lose people.

Almost every small business site I am asked to fix is failing at one of those three points, and usually not the one the owner expects.

Say what you do, in the words your customers use

The most common problem is not ugliness. It is vagueness. Headlines like "Empowering your potential" or "Solutions tailored to you" could belong to a physiotherapist, a software company or an accountant.

Your first line should be so plain it feels almost too obvious to you: what you do, who for, and where. "Custom and ready-made furniture in Ramat Beit Shemesh" is not a clever headline. It is a clear one, and it is the reason someone stays on the page.

There is a search benefit too. The words your customers actually type are the same plain words you should be writing, and if those words appear nowhere on your site, you will not be found for them.

One obvious next step, repeated

A site should have one primary action: book a call, request a quote, get in touch, order. Not five competing options, and not a phone number tucked in the footer.

That action should appear near the top, again in the middle, and again at the end, so nobody has to hunt for it or scroll back up. If the visitor has to think about how to contact you, a proportion of them will simply not bother.

Assume they are on a phone

Most people will see your site on a phone, often standing up, often distracted. That has real design consequences: tap targets big enough for a thumb, no wall of text, no menu that needs pinching to read, a phone number that dials when tapped, and images that do not take five seconds to load on mobile data.

Test it yourself on your own phone, on mobile data with wi-fi off, the way a real customer would. It is the single most revealing five minutes you can spend on your website, and almost nobody does it.

Speed is a feature

A slow site loses people before they see anything at all. The usual culprit is images: photographs straight off a camera or phone at several megabytes each, uploaded without resizing.

Resize images to the size they actually display at, save them properly compressed, and a heavy page becomes a fast one without any redesign. This is the cheapest performance improvement available to a small business site and it is routinely ignored.

Proof that you are real

People buy from businesses that look like they exist. Real photographs rather than stock images of strangers in a boardroom. Genuine testimonials with a name attached. Where you are based. A working phone number and email. If you have done work you are proud of, show it.

This matters even more in a small community, where a recommendation carries more weight than any advertising, and where the site's job is often to confirm what someone has already been told about you.

The technical bits that quietly matter

  • A unique title and description for every page. This is what shows up in Google's results. It is the closest thing to a free advert you have, and a surprising number of sites leave it as "Home".
  • Real pages, not one long scroll. A single page with everything on it can only rank for one thing. Separate pages for your main services can each rank for their own.
  • Links Google can follow. Navigation built from buttons and scripts instead of ordinary links is invisible to search engines. Plain links, every time.
  • A sitemap that matches reality. If it lists addresses that no longer exist, or misses your newest pages, you are making Google guess.
  • HTTPS. Non-negotiable now. Browsers warn people away from sites without it.

What to do if your site is already live

You do not need to start again. Work through it in this order, because it is roughly the order of return on effort:

  1. Rewrite the first screen so it says plainly what you do and who for.
  2. Put one clear call to action in three places on every page.
  3. Open it on your phone on mobile data and fix whatever annoys you.
  4. Compress every image over about 300KB.
  5. Give every page its own title and description.
  6. Split a long single page into real pages if you offer more than one distinct service.

That list will do more for most small business websites than a full redesign, and you can work through it over a few evenings.
