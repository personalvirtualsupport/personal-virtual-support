What does a virtual assistant actually do?

"Whatever you need" is a useless answer when you are trying to decide whether support is worth it. Here is the concrete version: the work I am actually asked to take on, and how to pick your first handover.

The honest answer: whatever is quietly eating your week

Ask ten business owners what a virtual assistant does and you will get ten different answers, because a virtual assistant is not really a job description. It is a way of getting the parts of your business that do not need you off your desk and onto someone else's, without hiring, without an office, and without a permanent salary.

That said, "whatever you need" is not much help when you are trying to work out whether it is worth it. So here is a concrete list: the work I am most often asked to take on, roughly in the order people hand it over.

1. Inbox and email

This is almost always first, and for good reason. It is where the pressure lives. In practice this means sorting what arrives, drafting replies for you to approve, answering the routine ones outright, and flagging the handful that genuinely need your judgement. Within a few weeks most people find their inbox has stopped being a source of dread and started being a list of decisions.

2. Calendar and scheduling

Booking, confirming, rescheduling, sending reminders, and making sure the day actually holds together. It sounds small until you count the messages it takes to move one meeting. Scheduling is also where mistakes are expensive: a missed appointment costs you the slot, the client's goodwill, and the time spent apologising.

3. Follow-up and chasing

The quiet killer. Quotes that never got a reply. Invoices that were never paid. Suppliers who said they would come back to you three weeks ago. Most business owners know exactly what is outstanding and simply never get to it. This is some of the highest-value work a virtual assistant does, because chasing an unpaid invoice pays for itself.

4. Documents, files and paperwork

Cleaning up a Drive folder that has become a junk drawer. Renaming files so they can be found. Building a proper folder structure and then keeping to it. Pulling together the paperwork for an accountant, a landlord, an insurer, or a due-diligence request. Unglamorous, and enormously calming once it is done.

5. Trackers and simple systems

A spreadsheet that shows you where every client, order or project actually stands. Not a piece of software you have to learn, just a clear view of the things you are currently holding in your head. Once it exists, someone else can update it, and that is the point.

6. Client communication

Sending the welcome message, the reminder, the "just checking you got everything" note. The touches that make a small business feel organised and cared-for, which almost never happen when the owner is doing everything.

7. Research and legwork

Comparing suppliers, finding a service, pulling together options with the prices and the trade-offs laid out so you can decide in five minutes instead of losing an evening.

8. Content and admin around your marketing

Formatting and scheduling an email, updating a page on your website, keeping a mailing list tidy, making sure the thing you wrote actually goes out. Not strategy: the execution that turns a good intention into something that shipped.

What a virtual assistant is not

Worth being straight about this. A virtual assistant is not a substitute for a specialist. I am not your accountant, your lawyer, or your developer, and anyone who tells you they can be all of those things is overselling.

A virtual assistant is also not a magic fix for a business with no systems at all. If everything lives in your head, the first month is partly about getting it out of your head. That is normal, and it is worth it, but it is not instant.

How to work out what to hand over first

You do not need a full job description to start. Try this instead: for one week, every time you catch yourself thinking "I really need to get to that", write it down. Do not filter. At the end of the week you will have a list, and the pattern in it will be obvious.

Then pick the one that meets two conditions: it is taking up real time, and it does not actually require you personally. That is your first handover. Not the hardest thing, not the most important thing, just the one with the best ratio of time saved to explanation needed.

Most people are surprised by how much falls into that category once they look honestly. The work that only you can do is usually a much smaller share of the week than it feels like.

A realistic first month

Week one is handover: you explain, I ask a lot of questions, and I write things down so you never have to explain them twice. Week two is usually messier than you expect, because that is when the gaps show up. By week three or four, the routine work is running without you and you have your attention back.

That is the actual deliverable. Not tasks completed, but the mental space you get back when you stop being the only person who knows what is outstanding.
